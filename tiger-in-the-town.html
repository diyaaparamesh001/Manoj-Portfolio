Tiger in the Town

By Manoj Ashodia

They had been together like a glove and a hand. Only in this case, the glove was a perfect fit. And by perfect, we don't mean a good fit or a precise fit; it was just the right fit, slightly lax at the edges, allowing breathing room. However, they did not seem like a perfectly fitting glove and its hand lately. A chasm between them had appeared, caused by the argument about a rogue tiger that had escaped the zoo. Was he set free by someone from the inside, or did he just get bored and decide to walk away just because he could?

Bala and Chanda were on opposite sides of the chasm, and it was getting wider by the day. Consequently, their bickering and random outbursts had reached a point where it threatened the glove would leave the hand and find itself another hand. A bad pregnancy, which was supposed to put a bridge on the chasm, only widened the gap.

Bala and Chanda, the black and the white, formed a perfect grey texture, living their lives to a compromising fulfillment despite being at loggerheads all the time, sharing a roof in the ghetto of the big city, where cats governed the nights and stray dogs the day. But these days, a tiger was ruling the cityscape.

Chanda was more inclined towards credulity and suffered social anxiety, and Bala was a sceptic and wild, like a restless monkey set free after months of confinement. She never stepped out of the house, while he was never inside the house, barring the nights. The nights they spent together, without fail. Only these days, Bala was a little cautious while stepping out of the house, because of the tiger.

Even then, the glove-hand theory agreed with them. It was a curious question the outsiders often asked! How the hell are they still together? The answer to this question lay around the time Bala growled about being hungry at 4 in the morning.

"Hey, I would really love it if you woke up and made me a sandwich; I am fucking starving," he said quietly, as if talking to himself, hoping that she was listening. She wasn't. She was snoring.

He kept turning sides on the bed, feeling miserable, and as a dull pain pierced the depths of his stomach, he sat upon the bed. Next to him, Chanda was deep inside a dreamless sleep. Her face looked completely lifeless. By then, he was used to her voice tearing through the silence of the nights and interrupting his sleep occasionally due to her affliction of sleep-talking. But Bala never bemoaned it, for he understood the weight of the unspoken words which her introverted disposition bore, that needed a way out one way or another. Introverted people often have the most active subconscious.

"Hey, my moon, hey. You need to wake up right now. Hey. Chanda." Bala patiently searched for any signs of movement. He touched her face; it was pleasantly warm, which was comforting. Outside, it was less than 2 degrees. Bala hated the winters as much as Chanda loved them.

He stood up and clamped shut the only window in the room, not before stealing a glance outside, hoping to finally spot the stray tiger that was rumoured to be as big as a buffalo. The fog blocked his vision. He could see no further than one foot.

The howling of winds had stopped, and the room now rested in complete silence. He looked at Chanda, still lifeless, with her pretty and warm face facing the ceiling, her left arm resting over her shapely, braless bosom, and the right one hanging on the right side of the bed.

Bala waited for her body to display some sign of life. His stomach yowled, his fingers shook thanks to his low blood sugar acting up, and he felt dizzy. Bala placed his hips on the numbing cold floor and wondered what could be causing this intense pang of hunger at 4 in the morning. It had never happened before, and it deserved careful introspection so that this abomination never happens again.

It was a normal Friday. I woke up at 7, left for work at 9, had an egg omelette with a tall glass of milk, reached the office, managed accounts until lunch, stole my co-worker's pasta for lunch, worked until 6, bought dinner from the grocery store, reached home by 7, read a book, had dinner, had sex, and slept by 1.

If the ordinary days had an ordinary day among them, it was this day.

Bala replayed the entire day once again, this time in deeper detail, from Chanda's point of view. He had all the time in the world anyway.

Chanda woke up shortly after me and cooked bread and an omelette. She then raced to finish the Dostoevsky book she had been wanting to for weeks now. Chanda never ate breakfast. 'Breakfasts are worthless; they change the sweet taste in the mouth that you have after a good sleep,' she once quipped. 'She only eats her breakfast after a bad night's sleep because she doesn't like how nightmares taste. More often than not, she sleeps well, and just before going to bed the previous night, she had said that she was going to have a great sleep, and she did not eat breakfast today, which means she did have a good night's sleep.

Bala decided to focus on this detail especially.

That said, she did sound a little cranky and not at all like someone who just had a great sleep. Maybe she had a bad sleep, yet she did not eat her breakfast to trick her mind into believing she had a great sleep. So that must be it. This was very Chandaeque.

Bala uttered and laughed a Balaesque laugh, which was a muffled screeching sound that sounded more like hiccuping than laughter.

He peeped at her once again; still no signs of life. He suddenly recalled the existence of leftover chicken from the dinner in the fridge. He quietly opened the door, climbed down the stairs, and rushed towards the kitchen, cursing himself over something, something he himself wasn't sure was a proper reason to curse himself for.

The chicken wasn't there. 'Chanda must have eaten it after I slept,' he thought and sighed.

This was another ongoing tussle between the two- they were never hungry at the same time.

Owing to a traumatic culinary experience, involving, but not limited to, a hospitalization, Bala and Chanda both had ruled that he was not a good cook. But then again, he hadn't felt this hungry in years. Another attempt could happen today without disrupting the order of the universe.

He walked over to the fridge, wide awake at 4:12 in the morning. The window above the sink overlooked the street, where the occasional sighting of the tiger was rather frequent, as per Chanda. Bala hadn't quite seen the tiger himself, but an ominous presence was always there, and he had no reason to doubt Chanda's claims.

Majestic, but scared nonetheless of the way the civilized world operated, the poor, scary cat must have contemplated retreating to his cell countless times.

Bala stuck his head inside the fridge again, like a curious cat studying a dumpster, and his eyes roamed in hopes of finding any of the leftover chicken from last night. There wasn't any. Bread: there was a stale piece of bread in the fridge, but no eggs to be seen anywhere. 'Chanda must have finished the leftover chicken and the eggs both,' he thought, 'and I had forgotten to buy eggs as she told me to.' He sat at the dining table with a cold water bottle between his palms. Yes, brewing a black coffee was on top of the list of things he could do for himself, and that was it. It was a short list, as compared to Chanda's. She could cook anything, even a tiger if she was at it.

Stealing temporary calm from a perspiring, cold steel bottle between his palms, he looked at the time. The clock screamed 4:15, and Chanda never woke up before 6:30 or 7:00. He tried to divert his mind and thought about the time Chanda boasted about taming a lion on an African safari and naming him Zoravar.

"Quite a motherly name for the animal that could've snapped your neck in less than a moment if it wanted," Bala had ridiculed her when he first heard the story, and he scoffed again today, just thinking of it. The train of thought moved from one deadly cat of the past to another one from the present.

Is there any chance the tiger actually got tired of the way he was being treated in the zoo? Maybe. But that would mean fuelling Chanda's incredulous behaviour, her habit of humanizing every other creature, especially the jungle cats.

He rose from the chair and snapped a quick look inside the fridge once again, hoping something would just magically appear out of nowhere, but nothing did. His stomach grumbled once again, this time a bit deeper and louder. Drinking half the bottle of cold water only intensified his hunger. He contemplated whether he could force himself back to sleep, but immediately discarded the notion. He was wide awake, like a soldier amidst the raining bullets.

Why would the zookeeper do such a vile thing, though- let a dangerous animal roam around openly in the city? The itch needed a deeper penetration, not only a surface-level scratch. He must have been bored, concluded Bala, and put an end to his musings for good. Chanda couldn't ever win this argument, like years earlier when Bala wanted her to abort, and she wanted to keep the child, which ended in tragedy.

When the clock struck 4:20, his woes seemed to have a possible end as he heard slow, tipsy footsteps approaching him from behind. He turned back. His wife, in her light blue nightgown, looked like the goddess of appetite in the dimmed yellow light of the kitchen.

"What's up with you? It's freezing outside, and you are sitting here, sipping cold water as if..." she did not find the right metaphor, took a deep breath, and sat down on the chair opposite him, rubbing her forehead. "I got scared for a moment when I didn't find you in bed."

"I'm ravenous, and I think I am going to die if I don't eat something soon," Bala said, trying to avoid eye contact.

Chanda's sleepy, droopy, salivafied face had all the signs of a bad night's sleep.

"Wow. That's unexpected after you went all rambo on chicken last night," she said, rising from her chair. She turned the stove on, then turned it off in response to the very next words that carelessly left the threshold of his mouth.

"But you ate the leftover chicken, didn't you?"

Chanda didn't say anything. She spread her thighs on the chair again. "I am in no mood to argue," she said, yawning.

The kitchen wrapped itself in silence.

Chanda's face displayed a very Chanda Face. The I-don't-give-a-fuck-and-you-are-almost-dead-to-me-so-fuck-off face. Eyes shut, deep inhalation, mulling over something in her head not a soul can tell what, inexpressive face. Bala knew this face as The Face. And he just knew that even if the tiger broke into the house right then and ate him right in front of her, it would still be a hassle for her to emote anything other than a resting bitch-face resulting from the nuisance of her already bad sleep being disrupted. But Bala's accusation was wrapped in some truth. He was determined. And he was indignant.

Chanda terminated her Chanda Face, and mellowed down.

"What about a bread omelette?" Chanda uttered blithely. Bala breathed deeply.

"There aren't any eggs in the fridge, and I genuinely think only meat can satisfy my craving," Bala replied confidently, staring right into her eyes.

She stood up and walked over to the fridge, leaned into the refrigerator, and from where did the eggs appear in her hands? That sure wasn't something to be asked of Chanda. Without looking back at her husband, Chanda turned towards the stove.

His eyes saw his wife in the yellow kitchen setting up a meal; his heart was seeing a bridge over the chasm, although precarious.

She had a cup of coffee made for herself first, and then broke the eggs into a large pan with hot oil already rolling in it, spreading its diameter as she tilted the pan left and right while sipping her coffee. Within a few minutes, the omelette was ready. Bala just watched, like a beggar watches a rich man swirling his fingers through his wallet!

She served the omelette on a steel saucer and sat on a chair across the table, facing her husband as he tore through the omelette like a ravenous dog. Chanda looked at her husband sweetly. As per her, although he was a far cry from being an ideal husband, he had his moments.

She swigged down her remaining coffee in one go, and he was done with the omelette, but his face didn't display satisfaction. Who would know that look better than his wife of 10 years? She conveyed a bewildered look.

"God, what has gotten into you today, poda patti," she cursed in Malayalam as she gulped down the last of her coffee, looking tired. Bala snapped at this point.

"I don't know; maybe if you had been humane enough not to finish the last of the chicken, we wouldn't have come this far, with me dying of hunger and you disrupting your precious sleep."

"Just stop right there", she yelled. Although those words came from anger, there was a subtle hint of remorse in her voice. After a brief pause, she continued.

"Just listen," she said while wiping off the coffee residue on her upper lip. "Something weird happened last night after you went to sleep," she said, looking down and joining her palms. "I have been having bad nights of sleep for a few weeks ever since that tiger thing became the news. I mean, yes, we still have a house and hence, we are safe and all, but something was bothering me, something about the tiger, that I couldn't pinpoint. I mean, we are having routine fights about whether the tiger was freed by the guard or whether it somehow found a way to unlock his cage and silently walked out of the zoo. I could not stop thinking about it for some reason. Last night was the same; around midnight, I came to the kitchen to drink water, and I heard a growl. It was not a warning or an angry sound; it was sad and lonely and hungry. I knew it was coming from outside. When I stared outside this window, I saw a shadow standing amidst the fog. It seemed like a big dog, but as it approached closer, I couldn't believe my eyes; it was the tiger. He stood there, outside our window, staring back at me. I can't put words to that feeling, but it was a very peculiar one, as if the tiger was calling out to me, and then, in the voice of a wise old man, the tiger began speaking. It was a human voice, creaking, trembling, but as clear as me and you talking." Chanda took a long sip from the water bottle. Bala was listening with his mouth half-open.

"Wait... hold on... so you're saying the tiger spoke, like you and me... in our language...?" Bala somehow suppressed an insolent fit of laughter.

"I know it's hard to believe, but I have no other way of explaining this to you. It happened whether you believe it or not. And my initial reaction was to shut down the window and go back to bed, hoping that it was a dream. You were lying beside me in deep sleep, and I didn't want to wake you up. But then, I couldn't keep my mind off that tiger, and I went back to the sink and opened the window, and there he was, still standing there, unmoved, and staring right into my eyes, and spoke again." Chanda was shivering. Bala couldn't tell if it was her story or the cold.

"And what did the tiger say to you?" Bala asked, still sporting the expression of disbelief and sarcasm.

"Dlihc, yrgnuh ma I dna dog eht ma I," Chanda said, staring at the window above the sink. "It sounded like gibberish. I didn't know what to say or do, standing there at the window, frozen. But the way the tiger looked at me, it was like a little child that looks at its mother when it's hungry."

"And…" Bala sighed. He had already concocted that his midnight dinner was fed to the tiger.

"So I threw the leftover chicken at him from the window, and within seconds, he grabbed the pieces and devoured them. Watching him gorge on the food reminded me of Zoravar in many ways, and I felt a motherly affection towards it."

Bala didn't know how to react.

"While you were at it, you should have asked whether he ran away on his own or was freed by the guard," Bala said sarcastically. Chanda knew it was a story no one would believe easily, and Bala was one of the staunchest realists she knew.

"I am sorry for feeding our food to the tiger, but I felt a sense of responsibility towards him. I could not think of anything else," Chanda said, hanging her head low.

"The tiger was, in fact, freed by the guard," Bala said in a low baritone. This rubbed Chanda the wrong way.

"Over the arguments we have had over the past few weeks, while I initially believed that the tiger walked out of his cage on his own, I was slowly coming to accept that I was wrong and that the tiger was in fact freed by the guard. But after what I experienced just a few hours ago, I am now completely certain that I was right from the beginning. This tiger could," her voice seemed determined. "I saw this look in the tiger's eyes that he would not harm another animal for his hunger. The streets are swarmed with so many stray dogs and cats, and yet, the tiger came straight to our house. The tiger is a non-violent saint. Just like Mahatma Gandhi."

"Just because you hallucinated a talking tiger, it doesn't mean a tiger has enough sense of rationality, self-awareness, or intelligence to accomplish such a task. It's a bloody tiger. All they want to do is fuck, eat, and sleep," Bala replied.

"Maybe he just manipulated the guard and had him open the gates for him. I am sure one or two guards at the Agakhan Palace Jail admired Gandhi enough to offer to help him escape at night. I mean, I remember feeling an inexplicable urge to do anything the tiger said, like I was taken aback by its personality, and furthermore, this way, it can be said that both of us were right and thus it can bring an end to our arguments. Maybe the tiger wanted to be free, and he only manipulated the guard to open the cage," Chanda said, rubbing her palms, hoping to land on an agreeable conclusion. But Bala, still struggling with intense cravings, was in a different mood.

"Or maybe it proves both of us were wrong," he said and bowed his head.

Chanda was soft by then and prepared herself another cup of coffee and asked Bala if he wanted one. Bala denied it as always. He had stopped drinking coffee years ago after he found out that caffeine in his system made him hungrier.

"Have you tried getting back to sleep?" She asked, sitting down with the gigantic coffee mug between her small palms.

Bala, still heads down, nodded his head.

"What about that newly opened place that is open 24x7? They must have something you'll like?" she said.

"The tiger is strolling around the city as if he owns it, and I don't think anything would be open at this time. The terror is so real."

"Tiger, after all, is the king of the jungle. Eh," said Chanda, sipping her coffee slowly, careful not to make a slurping sound.

"That's a lion, you stupid woman, who gets fooled by a tiger," he remarked with a very, very low voice, not loud enough to reach Chanda's beautiful, shapely ears.

"Okay, what do you want then?"

"I don't know, Chanda. I need to eat. I could eat you right now if you allowed me, if you weren't so damn cranky and stupid," he blurted out unconsciously. And only after the words had left his lips and reached her ears did he realise what doom was about to befall him.

"Wow, this is repugnant, even by your standards," she said, surprisingly calm.

"I am just being honest here... A fucking tiger ate my dinner, and my wife thinks it's his dead son reincarnated. This is fucking stupid…" he said, glaring sharply at her.

"Y… why…?" Words got jammed in Chanda's throat. Bala immediately realized the error in his careless words.

"I didn't mean… you need to cry, Chanda. Let it out. It was not your fault. Not mine either. Let's just forget about that. Learn to see a tiger as a tiger."

Chanda was startled. Her moist eyes stared at the breadcrumbs on the saucer.

She stood up and prepared herself one more cup of coffee, her third in a row.

"Chanda?"

Chanda was silent.

"I am hungry, baby."

Silence.

"Do something, I feel like I am gonna die".

Silence.

She had already poured the coffee and was just standing above the sink. But after a closer look, he realized that she was actually staring out of the window.

"Let's go out and see if there is anything we can find," she said.

"What if we don't find anything?"

"Then we find the tiger, and we eat the tiger!"

Bala's eyes widened, and for a moment, he felt dizzy.

"Please tell me that you are just having a bad-humour day."

"Why not? Why can't we eat the tiger? And nobody cares about the tiger anyway; it has been weeks and not a single person from the authorities has come to rescue it. They're probably waiting for the tiger to kill someone so that they look heroic in the public's eyes," Chanda said, still staring into the fog outside.

"Not a minute ago, you were talking about feeling a motherly affection towards it. And now you are determined to kill and eat the tiger?" He stated the obvious and then continued. "And also, is it even legal?"

Chanda rolled her eyes and took a long sip of the coffee. "Probably not. But I have often wondered what a tiger would taste like. And this particular tiger seems interesting, and isn't it quite human to eat any living thing that seems interesting?" Chanda said.

Bala stood up and tapped on the dining table twice, and continued talking.

"That reminds me of an interesting conversation I had with a co-worker the other day. Because the tiger eats people, wouldn't that make us cannibals of sorts? I mean, look at what they say: you are what you eat. So if a tiger eats a person, it becomes a person. And we eat a tiger, we eat a person. And we eat a person, we are cannibals. And cannibalism is illegal, right?" His eyes wandered around for a few moments, and then he continued, "I was thinking about this paradox for a few days. Now that I have said it aloud, I realise how stupid this sounds. My bad, sorry. And when it comes to that, I am beginning to realise that maybe you are right. A tiger couldn't be kept hostage if he didn't want it. But isn't that a contradiction? I mean, why would he agree to become a hostage in the first place if he could be freed anytime at his will?"

Chanda smiled at her husband, for the first time since her son died in childbirth, the one she planned to name Zoravar. She gulped down the coffee in one go and cleaned her mouth with the back of her left hand. "Maybe he thought it would be a unique experience. Like he was feeling lazy one fine night and didn't feel like sleeping or anything. And decided to mix things up a bit, as if he was free to act however way he wanted," she stopped.

"Isn't that really wonderful?" Bala said and continued. "A tiger aware of its rights, of the concept of free will, rising above his animalistic instincts of sleeping, killing, eating, fucking, eating, sleeping and so on. Generally speaking, a tiger with self-awareness can be a sign that God in fact does exist! I mean, look at it this way. Vatican church denies the possibility of life on other planets because they believe God only 'blessed' our planet with life, and on our planet, God only blessed the sense of self-awareness to human beings because we are superior to all other species on the planet just because we are created in his image. They would deny anything contradicting this belief," Bala said, excited.

"And a talking tiger could cause the Church to be shut down," Chanda said with equal excitement.

"This is taking it too far. But it can be possible..." Bala said, sinking back into the chair.

"Precisely!" replied Chanda.

"So we eat the tiger... or better yet, we get him to talk again and destroy the idea of God and the church?" Chanda continued.

"Are you an idiot? This fucking tiger ate the chicken that was meant for me; hence the tiger became that chicken, and hence, I have to eat the tiger, or this hunger will never be satisfied," Bala said with a solid determination.

"So, we eat the tiger!" he exclaimed.

"We eat the king of the jungle. I bet it tastes like chicken," she smiled.

"It's a lion. A lion is the king of the jungle," Bala said, this time out loud.

"So what's a tiger?" Chanda asked.

"A tiger to the jungle is what Mahatma Gandhi was to the Indian freedom struggle," Bala winked.

They both smiled at each other; it was a warm smile. A smile that builds a solid bridge.

As Bala and Chanda barged out into the open together with their hunting gear, not afraid of the tiger anymore, the sleepy sun smiled at them deep in the east.
