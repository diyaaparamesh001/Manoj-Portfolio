The Haryana Story

By Manoj Ashodia · New Delhi, India

To what extent can the popular Haryanvi 'gangster music' be attributed to the stereotypical image of the Haryanvi populace? Or is the regional art just amplifying what's already there? Let's brood.

When I walked in on my 13-year-old cousin cranking a brazenly violent Haryanvi song in an ear-piercingly loud sound, my immediate reaction was one of concern. The singer, a regional talent, audaciously sang: 'ek khatolla jail ke bheetar, ek khatolla jail ke bahar' (I have one bed in the prison and a second one outside).

My cousin knew the lyrics by heart and was showing off to me by humming them along. His playlist was laden with such bawdy, violent songs dropping swag on every beat that you can't help but marvel at the gusto of such artists with no fear of consequences. And this 'Khatolla' guy definitely knew the 'formula', because the song is currently sitting at 45 million views on YouTube.

I would be lying if I told you I was startled at the gauche lyrics. They spoke of nothing I hadn't heard randomly on the streets of Haryana, or the roadways buses, or my family members quarreling among each other throughout my growing up years. But my 13-year-old cousin's fascination with it rubbed me the wrong way. I was way past the age of this influence, but what about him? How long until his subconscious accepts it as the 'cool thing' and it metastasizes into a full-blown behavioural disposition later in his life?

It got me thinking. When I was growing up, the popular music was raagnis, an old traditional folk form of Haryanvi music that flourished on the back of beautiful poetry and moral and ethical lessons. But did they ever even leave a dent in the zeitgeist of Haryanvi society and make it poetic at heart? No. Rather the opposite.

So then, is it a futile conversation whether art impacts society in any way? Let us dig deeper into the matter.

Chicken and Egg Situation

We can debate endlessly on whether art influences society or vice versa. The artists I am so strictly against are a product of the same society. They grew up in the same primordial khichdi of conditioning, behavioural quirks, language, and the cultural symbols that they later adapted in their music. The citizenry found relatability in the music, and it goes on in circles. It's hard to find a perpetrator in this case, so then, from which direction should the winds of change begin to flow- the society or the artists?

Bollywood and stand-up comics have never missed the opportunity to pull jokes on them either. Most members of the community unironically rejoice in this mocking stereotyping, not because they don't get the irony, but because for them it reinforces the idea that Haryanvis need to be feared.

Two of the biggest artists from the land, Randeep Hooda and Jaideep Ahlawat, earned most popularity by playing no-nonsense, brazen, and blunt Haryanvis in Laal Rang, and Pataal Lok respectively. That itself speaks a lot. And then the cycle continues. Hooda and Ahlawat portrayed many other characters that were sensitive male figures on-screen, and yet none of them became memorable for the people of Haryana.

As a result, for most people from the state, that aggression becomes their only collective identity, something bigger than themselves. And hence, it lives on.

And the booming music industry is furthering this fake sense of pride, which harms them more than it serves them. The result is a feared reputation on the streets of Delhi and Haryana, increasing gang violence and more youth inclining towards this attitude, which sometimes costs them their future.

But when it comes to the majority, what's the harm, right?

A group of people want to be feared; their favourite music further reinforces their belief. Win-win!

But what about the other side of Haryanvis?

Not From Here Neither There

When I moved out of Haryana and into the intellectual circles, I was received either with fear or confusion. 'Are you really from Haryana?' was the most frequent jibe at my occasional accent slips. Even the people who had never encountered a Haryanvi were shocked to see a non-aggressive Haryanvi who looked like a well-behaved, decent person, and who wrote poetry. I would defensively cite examples from artists such as Dada Lakhmichand and Azaad Singh Khandakhedi, two legendary poets from Haryana. It turned out they were muted amidst the loud, deafening music of modern Haryanvi pop. Nobody knew about them. Out of shame, I began to cover up my background and found a sense of validation when someone pointed out that I don't look or talk like a Haryanvi.
