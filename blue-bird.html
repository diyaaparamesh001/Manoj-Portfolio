The Blue Bird

By Manoj Ashodia · Ratangarh, Uttarakhand

As Santram dug his aching back into the comfortable wooden chair that was conveniently curved from behind, and took the first sip of the evening chai, he reminisced about all the times he purposely delivered the letters to the wrong addresses. On the cushioned antique sandalwood chair, Santram slowly sipped his tea made from the finest tea leaves his house-help Ravi could find in the small town of Ratangarh, located in the bosom of the gorgeous Indian state of Uttarakhand. It was 3 weeks since Santram took permanent retirement from his hectic day job as a postman at the biggest Post Office in the biggest metropolitan in northern India. Yet, sitting in his freshly mowed lawn, inhaling the calming scent of the green hillside, Santram's brain still wasn't at rest. Right from his childhood, to his adulthood, to his middle-age, he was bombarded with a lot of voices. The very familiar voices of his parents, then his wife, then his son and now, his guilty self.

His remorse didn't as much stem from the fact that he could never fulfill his childhood dreams of reaching the stars, becoming an astronaut, then a science fiction writer; it was more from leading a life that was as inconsequential as that of the bird sitting on the mango tree across his lawn, on the street. Santram gazed at the gorgeous blue-feathered bird and wondered what purpose this bird serves in this world? What could have been the driving force for the evolution to think of birthing another feathery creature that didn't contribute anything to the intricate machinery this world is. But then again, what does it matter, in the grand scheme of things, he wondered, everything is pointless anyway.

He took a good whiff oozing out of the fresh Assam leaves that were crushed and mixed with boiling cow-milk to create this sublime beverage that he had been addicted to since his early years. Santram always believed that chai is among the very few 'good' things that the world had invented and everyone deserved to develop a taste for it. He momentarily felt bad for all the lactose-intolerants out there in the world, devoiding themselves of one of the actual finer things in life. It took him a second from feeling bad for the vegans and dairy-haters, to think of the time his father beat him half-dead when he was 13 years old. Santram had stolen 50 paise from his father's pyjamas to buy himself a new set of fantasy books that a local hawker was selling for cheap. With every blow his father laid on the teenager, he thought he should have stolen more, to get more books, as it would have landed him up into this trouble anyway. He promised himself to steal 1 rupee the next time his father drinks himself to sleep, completely oblivious to the material world.

Sitting in his brown dyed cushioned chair, stroking his salt and pepper beard with one hand, Santram scrutinized all four pockets he had, with his left hand- two on his cotton shirt, and two on his olive linen pants. All he could find was a 100 rupee note which had accidentally remained unattended and had been washed with the pants the last time Ravi got the laundry done. Deeming the semi-crushed currency bill worthless at this moment, Santram shouted "RAVI" at the top of his lungs. Ravi came rushing into the lawn, from the inside, where he was probably studying for his mathematics exam due next week. He always wanted to be an actor but never had the means to move to a bigger city and join the theatre. He often dreamed of riches, but never knew his way to them. However, he always knew that Santram was the father figure he never had and often wondered if he would ever adopt a lower caste boy like him.

"Do you have a rupee coin?" asked Santram politely.

Ravi's nervous gaze met the 100 rupee bill in his master's left hand and the teacup in his right. He stole a quick glance at the cup, which was half-empty now. He sighed and quickly inspected the pockets of his half-pant and brought out a freshly minted 1 rupee coin. A glint suddenly emerged in Santram's tired eyes and he extended the 100 rupee bill towards the young boy in his early 20s.

"Can you exchange that for this?"

Ravi stood there for a moment, pondering about his master's mental well-being. But saying NO was out of the question. So, he simply nodded and took the 100 rupee bill from his master and gave him the 1 rupee coin. Santram stared at the coin for a good half-minute, as if trying to read the very tiny engravings on it. He spat on the coin a little and rubbed it on his thighs.

"That'll be all," said Santram. Ravi quickly stuffed the 100 rupee bill inside his left pocket and rushed back inside.

Santram stared at the coin for a good minute-or-so and put it in his shirt pocket. He peeked inside his cup; he was halfway through the chai, which was miraculously still warm enough to soothe his sore throat. He took yet another sip while gaping at the blue feathered strange bird that was unmoved since he last looked at it. Santram fantasized about a silent message his estranged son had probably sent to his father from thousands of miles away. This bird was not usually seen in these hills and this realization further allowed Santram to delve into the pleasant thought of his son finally deciding to talk to his father, in his own unusual style. Arjun was always a weird young man. When he was 15 or so, after reading a pirated copy of 'Mein Kampf', he proclaimed that he was going to become a dictator like Hitler. The innocent ambition was laughed off at that time, but the seeds that were sowed that day grew into a lush green tree that was strongly determinant and had great clarity of thought. Arjun left his home, joined politics. The last time Santram heard of his only son, he was cleaning toilets for the rich in a Middle-Eastern country.

Santram missed his son and often regretted the conversation they had that day when his 20-year-old well-built son asked to be enrolled into the Defence Academy. His father hated the armed forces and thought they were just brainless zombie puppets of the ruling regime and that he should opt for a career as a writer. Arjun had no interest in becoming a writer and the same night this conversation took place, he ran away, stealing 1000 rupees from his father's khaki linen pants he wore to work every day. Santram often pondered if things would have been a little different had the conversation taken a slight detour, in a land where both father and son stood on no man's land, agreeing to disagree. Santram's beautiful wife couldn't handle the trauma and passed away the same night. The post-mortem report mentioned 'extreme grief resulting in a heart attack' as the cause, but Santram always doubted the reports. His wife was enough strong-willed to take a few emotional blows to her heart. The next day that she died, Santram delivered most letters he had all his life, none of them to wrong addresses.

Silently sipping the tea, Santram realized that sometime during his daze, the bird had flown away, perhaps to deliver another heartwarming message to another weary old soul sitting in his lawn, relaxing, with a cup of Assam chai in his hands. He peeked into his cup again, a couple of sips were left, and yet, the cup was still warm. Santram felt weird. He closely inspected the cup, which was gifted to him by a strange man on the streets when Santram was on his way to meet his ailing father. His father had died before he could see his son one final time. But Santram had no regrets. He performed the last rites, performed a son's duties and went back to the biggest metropolitan in Northern India and continued delivering letters.

Santram's eyes lifted up and again scrutinized the mango tree for any signs of another blue feathery messenger bird. But his attempts were futile. He moved his head around and took a good look at his freshly mowed land. Ravi was a hardworking man, a locale who helped Santram set this place up when he purchased this property a month back, investing all his life's savings into it. Ravi liked the tea he made but avoided drinking it during his exams.

Santram stood up, and once again, he peeked into his cup, one sip left. He thought of finishing the chai but then decided against it. Carefully, he bent down and placed the cup on the chair. His back was especially achy today. He cursed a bit and slowly straightened his spine.

He strolled into his lawn, but felt uneasy. His eyes were turning blurry with each passing moment. 'A mild dizziness', he thought and kept walking. Suddenly his legs cramped hard, as if a bulldozer had just rolled over them, and he fell to the green misty grass which smelled like petrichor. He wanted to shout for Ravi but his voice was trapped inside as if his throat was being clutched by two strong invisible hands. He began shaking frantically and saw the world around him getting hazier. His 55 years flashed in front of his eyes, even the parts which he never thought he remembered. He was watching a VHS tape of his life, but in fast-forward, only slowing down at the parts that mattered. The father, the son, the wife, all the disappointed people who got delivered the wrong letters! And then, the tape stopped, and he gave up struggling. He felt numbness taking over his limp body as she shut his eyes.

Into the dark, he thought of an alternate world, where he was sitting in the same lawn, on the same chair, with a heavy typewriter in his lap, writing the Great Indian Novel he always wanted to write, about a young man who jumped from a cliff, thinking it was the only way to learn to fly. Santram was yet to figure out the ending, but he always thought he would come up with something in the process of writing his novel.

Ravi came rushing from the inside, began sobbing, screaming loudly for help. As Santram dragged his last breath, he forced a smile, as he finally knew why the tea tasted extra delicious today.
